Appendix 1: Group charter

This charter records how Group 6 (Tutorial 1, Tanzeem) worked together on the EWB Challenge website. It was written up in October 2026 to describe the team’s actual way of working.

Purpose. The team worked together to design a shared rainwater system for Port Stewart and to present it clearly, with the Lama Lama community and the Yintjingga Aboriginal Corporation at the centre of the work.

Participation and workload. Every member took part and shared the workload. The team agreed who would take each section and divided the work fairly. The Week 10 presentation roles were Irene (background, current practices and existing solutions), Nusaibah (design criteria, design solution and presentation design), Zaheen (problem description, user needs and how the design meets them), Zihao (prototype, implementation and costings) and Wenrui (solution components, other considerations and recommendations). Contributions to the website are listed in the Teamwork Declaration (Table 2).

Communication. The team worked in the weekly tutorial and online. A group chat was used for updates, questions, drafts and decisions. Members replied quickly, which kept the work moving between classes. The team did not hold separate in-person meetings.

Decision-making. Each member shared their own ideas before a decision was made. The team decided together once everyone had contributed, and agreed decisions were recorded in the group chat.

Standards. Members agreed to keep the work accurate, to cite sources, and to represent the Lama Lama people and community respectfully.

Changes made and next tests

The rainfall assumption was lowered to about 1,200 mm. The filter evidence suggests one design change to test: slow-sand filters work best at a steady flow, so the filter should run slowly and continuously into a small treated-water tank feeding the UV unit and outlet (National Drinking Water Clearinghouse, 2000). Field studies also show filters underperforming and water being recontaminated in storage (Fiore et al., 2010), so UV, outlet hygiene and Ranger training matter. Next tests are a pilot slow-sand filter through the wet and dry seasons, laboratory tests of the commissioned system, and a check against Port Stewart rainfall records.

Proposed testing plan for the full-size system (for discussion)
  • Pilot test. Run a small pilot filter first, because slow-sand filter performance cannot be predicted from the raw water alone (National Drinking Water Clearinghouse, 2000).
  • Before first use. Run the filter to waste until it is mature. Have a laboratory test water from the commissioned system for E. coli, turbidity, pH and the chemical risks from roofs and pipes. Ask the UV supplier for evidence of the dose their unit delivers.
  • Routine checks. Trained Rangers check the UV indicator and battery, look at the water, and inspect the first-flush device and screens at least every six months and after big storms, noting each check in a logbook.
  • Regular water tests. E. coli tests at the tap on a schedule agreed with Queensland Health, with occasional laboratory samples.
  • If a test fails. Stop drinking from the outlet, use the Ranger Base or carted water, find the cause, fix it and re-test before restarting.
  • Safeguard to specify. A UV unit with an alarm and automatic shut-off so untreated water cannot flow if the lamp or power fails.

Proposal only. Not yet agreed with Queensland Health, the Yintjingga Aboriginal Corporation or a supplier.

Appendix 6: Prototype build, test and results

The full-size system cannot be built within this course, and arrangement, sizing and treatment carry the biggest design risks. The team therefore prototyped three things: the sizing model (virtual), the roof catch, tank fill and first-flush calculations (virtual), and a bench-scale filter (physical). Each answers one question. Do the tank and roof sizes meet demand? How does water move through the year? Does a sand and carbon filter make muddy water clearer?

Prototype A: virtual sizing model

The website’s sizing calculator was run six times, changing one input at a time. The base case is five homes with 80 m² of roof each, 1,200 mm of rain a year, a runoff coefficient of 0.8 and 20 people using 50 L a day.

What it shows. The two 10,000 L tanks meet about 53% of yearly demand and run empty from May to November. Changing rainfall, demand or roof size moves this between 50% and 63%, and doubling storage lifts it only to 59%. About 190,000 L of storage would meet all demand. The system gives a strong wet-season supply and a partial dry-season supply, and does not replace other sources in a long dry season. Limits. The model uses monthly averages for one typical year, assumes the tanks start full and the roofs are clean, and does not model cyclone damage, filter blockage or water quality. Modification. The runs did not change the layout. They changed how the design is described: the tanks meet about half of yearly demand and run empty from about May, so the Ranger Base and carted water fill the gap, and extra tanks are the most effective future upgrade.

Prototype B: bench-scale filter

Build. A clear plastic bottle was cut in two, and the top half was inverted over the lower half as a funnel and supported with paddle-pop sticks (Figure 14). The layers from the top are gravel, fine sand, coarse sand, granular activated carbon and cotton balls. The carbon was not rinsed.

Method. Each trial poured 500 mL of muddy water through the same filter. The team recorded the volume collected and the time taken, and rated clarity by eye on a scale from 1 (opaque brown) to 5 (clear). Trial 1 used dry media; trials 2 and 3 used the same filter with the media already wet.

Table 17. Bench filter results.
TrialPoured in (mL)Collected (mL)Retained or lost (mL, approx.)Share collectedTime for 500 mL

Clarity. The team rated the water 1 before filtering and 4 after, in trial 3 (Figure 12). The filtered water looked similar in clarity in all three trials.

What it shows. In all three trials the muddy water came out visibly clearer. Once the media were wet the filter lost less water and ran faster. The faster flow did not make the water look cloudier. The team’s explanation is that most of the cotton absorbed water in trial 1. Limits. This tests clarity only. Granular activated carbon was in the bed, but nothing was tested for chemicals or bacteria, so the water is not shown to be safe to drink. A real slow-sand filter also depends on a biological layer that takes time to form, which a short bench test does not reproduce. Only one filter and three trials were run, the rating was given by the team and not by an instrument, and the photograph is from the best trial. Modification. Dry media, especially the cotton, soaked up water in trial 1. For a real system the filter should be pre-wetted, or the first fill run to waste, before collecting drinking water, with extra volume allowed for the first fill.

What it shows. Rain exceeds demand only from December to March, and the two tanks fill in roughly one to two weeks at the January to March rates, so storage is the limit and not roof area. In the sizing runs, doubling storage adds 6 percentage points of demand met, while cutting the roofs by a quarter removes 3. First flush. 1 mm of rain over 1 m² is 1 L, so a first flush of 1 to 2 mm over an 80 m² roof diverts 80 to 160 L per roof, or 400 to 800 L per rain event across five roofs. That is 2% to 4% of the 20,000 L of storage. The 1 to 2 mm depth is a team assumption.
