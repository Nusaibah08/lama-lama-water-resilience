Why rainwater and not the alternatives

Design criteria

Seven criteria were used to judge the options. Each comes from the design brief and the project’s sources, and each is tied to what the community needs.

Table 3. Design criteria, what each means and why it matters.
CriterionWhat it meansWhy it matters
Safe waterMeets the Australian Drinking Water Guidelines and avoids the river’s metals.The river has shown arsenic and manganese, and most residents no longer trust it (Queensland Department of Environment and Science, 2021; Engineers Without Borders Australia, 2026a).
Seasonal reliabilityWater is available in both the wet and dry seasons.The river floods then dries up, and the dry-season population nearly doubles (Water Source, 2018; Prestipino, 2024).
Cyclone resilienceWithstands cyclone conditions and can be shut down beforehand.A cyclone destroyed the original supply in 2006 (Engineers Without Borders Australia, 2019).
Low maintenanceNeeds minimal power and chemicals and can be looked after with local skills.The community can be cut off for months, and Rangers already maintain solar systems (Engineers Without Borders Australia, 2026a).

The team looked at four ways to give Port Stewart safe water. Each is scored against the design criteria in the decision matrix below.

01 / 04

Repair the existing river filter

It already failed once and keeps breaking down during the dry season, and most residents have stopped trusting and using this water. (Engineers Without Borders Australia, 2026a)

02 / 04

Bore intake with aeration

The 2020 Arup and EWB design. It works at nearby Wujal Wujal, but needed a large amount of construction funding all at once, which is why it was never built. (Engineers Without Borders Australia, 2021)

03 / 04

Chlorination needs regular chemical deliveries into a community that is cut off for months each wet season. (Engineers Without Borders Australia, 2026a)

04 / 04

Proposed solution

Avoids the river entirely, can be built in stages, needs no chemicals and uses proven technology. (Kalt et al., 2014; PV Magazine Australia, 2020)

Is the water safe to drink?

Safe by design, not yet proven by testing. Each step is backed by published guidance, but the full system has not been built or tested for chemicals or bacteria. Roof rainwater can carry bacterial and protozoan pathogens (Ahmed et al., 2011), so every step matters and the system must be tested before anyone drinks from it.

What it deals with

Dust, leaves and bird droppings washed off roofs.

What the research says

First-flush diverters are recommended in Australian guidance (enHealth, 2010), and Queensland requires at least 20 L discarded and devices checked every six months (Queensland Government, 2009).

What it does not do

Does not remove germs already in the tank, and a blocked diverter stops working.

What it deals with

Animals, insects and mosquitoes entering the tanks.

What the research says

Queensland rules require mosquito-proof screens or flap valves on every tank opening (Queensland Government, 2009).

What it does not do

Does not stop sediment building up, so tanks still need cleaning.

What it deals with

Bacteria, protozoa, some viruses and cloudiness.

What the research says

Studies report 2 to 3 log removal of E. coli, 1.5 to 2 log of viruses and over 5 log of Cryptosporidium (Hijnen et al., 2004).

What it does not do

Needs a ripening period, works best at a steady flow and with clear water, and only partly removes arsenic (National Drinking Water Clearinghouse, 2000).

What it deals with

Bacteria, viruses and protozoa that pass the filter, with no chemicals.

What the research says

UV readily inactivates bacteria and protozoa, and viruses are the most resistant (Hijnen et al., 2006), so it follows the filter. A solar UVA unit reached a 4-log E. coli reduction in a different design (Kalt et al., 2014).

What it does not do

Leaves no lasting disinfectant, needs power and clear water, and needs the supplier’s proof of its dose.

What it deals with

Whether the other barriers are working.

What the research says

No E. coli should be detected in any 100 mL sample, and detection needs immediate action (National Health and Medical Research Council, n.d.). Rainwater needs “low maintenance, not no maintenance” (SA Health, n.d.).

What it does not do

Not done yet. The team has not tested water from this system, and testing is required before anyone drinks from it.

Source: Engineers Without Borders Australia (2026a).

The prototype: modelled and bench-tested

The team prototyped the design in two ways: a design model (the layout in Figure 10 and the sizing calculation below) and a bench-scale filter tested with muddy water. The full-size system has not been built. Change any input to see what the model does.

What was modelled, and how

The team modelled how the parts fit together for one cluster of five homes (Figure 10) and whether the roofs and tanks are the right size (the calculator and Figure 11), because arrangement and sizing carry the biggest design risks. The calculator runs a month-by-month water balance using the Bureau of Meteorology’s rainfall for Coen, about 60 km away (Bureau of Meteorology, n.d.-a; n.d.-b), and team assumptions for one cluster of about five homes, 20 people, 80 m² of roof per home, a runoff coefficient of 0.8 and 50 L per person per day.

What the model shows

Coen’s mean rainfall is about 1,200 mm a year, so the roofs could collect about 384,000 L in an average year against about 365,000 L of demand, a margin of only 5%. Two 10,000 L tanks fill in the wet season and run empty from about May until December. They would meet about 53% of demand (about 195,000 L), leaving about 170,000 L for the Ranger Base and carted water. Meeting all demand would need about 190,000 L of storage, which supports building in stages.

Bench filter test

To test the treatment steps, the team built a bench-scale filter from plastic bottles, with gravel, sand, granular activated carbon and cotton layers (Figure 14, Appendix 6), and poured 500 mL of muddy water through it three times. In every trial the water came out visibly clearer: in trial 3 the team’s clarity rating rose from 1 (opaque brown) to 4 (clear) (Figure 12). The dry media held back 150 mL in trial 1. Once the media were wet, 88% and 90% of the water was collected and the time fell from 8 min 19 s to 4 min 17 s (Table 6). For a real system, the filter should be pre-wetted or the first fill run to waste.
